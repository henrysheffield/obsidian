# How to Write a Great Essay (TEEL Framework)

> [!abstract] 
> An essay is a roadmap. It must follow a single, logical path where every single paragraph advances **one clear idea**. If a sentence doesn't actively support that specific idea, cut it.

**GREAT THING TO READ: ** 

[[Synonyms for 'shows']]

## 🛠️ The 6-Step Pipeline

### 1. Analyse the Question

Before writing, break the prompt down into three specific components to lock in your scope:

- **Content Terms:** The core concepts or themes specific to the task.
    
- **Limiting Terms:** The specific boundaries or scope the topic focuses on.
    
- **Directive Terms:** The action words telling you _how_ to write (e.g., _Analyse_, _Discuss_, _Evaluate_).
    

### 2. Define the Argument

- Formulate a precise thesis statement.
    
- _Bad Example:_ Writing generally about themes.
    
- _Good Example:_ "The harsh light within the text serves to amplify authority and project a god-like image of the priest."
    

### 3. Deploy Evidence

- **No Shopping Lists:** Never just list facts, quotes, or statistics. You must actively evaluate and explain _how_ the evidence proves your argument.
    
- **Contextual Linking:** Anchor abstract claims to concrete actions.
    
    - _Application:_ In _Twelve Angry Men_, the claustrophobic, hot day serves as a physical catalyst for the emotional hostility of the jurors, specifically mirroring the prejudice of Juror 10.
        

### 4. Structuring via TEEL

#### The Introduction (The Roadmap)

- **Context:** Provide a general statement of the topic to ground the reader.
    
- **Thesis:** Explicitly state your core argument (_"This essay argues that..."_).
    
- **Signposting:** Outline your 2–3 body arguments in one simple, highly concise sentence. This forms the exact sequence the essay must follow.
    

#### The Body Paragraphs (The Core)

Every single body paragraph must follow the **TEEL** structure and focus on **only one idea**.

- **T - Topic Sentence:** A clear, concise sentence signposting the single argument of the paragraph.
    
- **E - Explanation:** Elaborating and expanding on your core point.
    
- **E - Evidence:** Integrating specific quotes or textual examples to justify the claim.
    
- **L - Link:** Binding the argument back to your primary thesis statement.
    

#### The Conclusion (The Bigger Picture)

- **Rephrase:** Restate your original thesis using completely fresh language.
    
- **Synthesize:** Blend your main arguments together—do not just repeat them as a list.
    
- **The "So What?" Factor:** Explain the broader real-world implications of your argument.
    
    - _Application:_ _Twelve Angry Men_ ultimately functions as a direct allegory for the paranoid political landscape of the 1950s McCarthy trials.
        
- ⚠️ **Strict Bans:** Never introduce brand new material, and completely ban filler phrases like _"In conclusion"_.
    

### 5. Clarity & Polishing

- **High-Value Vocabulary:** Inject sophisticated words deliberately, not just to sound smart. Use terms like:
    
    - _Cogent_ (convincing)
        
    - _Ubiquitous_ (present everywhere)
        
    - _Dichotomy_ (a sharp contrast)
        
    - _Mitigate_ (to lessen or alleviate)
        
- **The 10-Minute Cold Read:** After finishing, step away. Then, read the essay from the perspective of an outsider who knows absolutely nothing about the topic.
    

> [!tip] The Cold Read Checklist
> 
> 1. Is the argument explicitly clear in the introduction?
>     
> 2. Does the actual structure match the roadmap laid out in the intro?
>     
> 3. Does each paragraph strictly represent **one** single idea?
>     
> 4. Does every single sentence support that specific paragraph's idea?
>     
> 5. Is the grammar flawless and the evidence logical?
>     

### 6. Sourcing

- For senior academic writing, cite every source using strict **APA Format**.
