| **Word**              | **Meaning & Example**                                                                                                                                                                                                                                 |
| --------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **exaltation**        | A feeling or state of extreme happiness, exhilaration, or elevation in rank/honor.<br><br>  <br><br>_Example:_ The crowd watched in pure exaltation as the team lifted the championship trophy.                                                       |
| **cognomen**          | An extra name given to an ancient Roman citizen (like a nickname); by extension, any surname or moniker.<br><br>  <br><br>_Example:_ Marcus Aurelius Antoninus is famously known by his cognomen, "The Philosopher."                                  |
| **verissimus**        | Most true, most truthful, or most genuine (Latin, often used as an honorific for sincerity).<br><br>  <br><br>_Example:_ Emperor Hadrian playfully called young Marcus "Verissimus" because of his inability to lie.                                  |
| **admonitions**       | Gentle or friendly reproofs; firm warnings or advice regarding behavior.<br><br>  <br><br>_Example:_ He filled his journals with daily admonitions to keep himself grounded and disciplined.                                                          |
| **insubstantiality**  | The quality of lacking substance, reality, or physical solidity; weakness.<br><br>  <br><br>_Example:_ The stoic reflected on the insubstantiality of fame, viewing it as mere smoke.                                                                 |
| **aphorisms**         | Pithy observations that contain a general, concise truth.<br><br>  <br><br>_Example:_ The book is packed with timeless aphorisms like "Living is more like wrestling than dancing."                                                                   |
| **pithy**             | Concise and forcefully expressive; full of vigor and substance.<br><br>  <br><br>_Example:_ She was famous for her pithy remarks that cut straight to the heart of the issue.                                                                         |
| **cistern**           | A tank or reservoir for storing water, especially under a house or underground.<br><br>  <br><br>_Example:_ Rainwater was collected in the stone cistern to ensure the villa had water through the dry summer.                                        |
| **indissoluble**      | Incapable of being undone, broken, dissolved, or destroyed; permanently binding.<br><br>  <br><br>_Example:_ The two philosophers shared an indissoluble bond forged by years of mutual hardship.                                                     |
| **macrocosm**         | The whole of a complex structure, especially the world or the universe, contrasted with a small part.<br><br>  <br><br>_Example:_ Stoics believe that human society should reflect the harmony of the cosmic macrocosm.                               |
| **microcosm**         | A community, place, or situation regarded as encapsulating in miniature the characteristic qualities of something much larger.<br><br>  <br><br>_Example:_ The small, self-contained village was a microcosm of the entire nation.                    |
| **effeminate**        | (Of a man) having characteristics regarded as typical of a woman; unmanly (often used historically as a criticism of softness).<br><br>  <br><br>_Example:_ Ancient Roman critics often dismissed a love for excessive luxury as an effeminate trait. |
| **epigram**           | A pithy saying or remark expressing an idea in a clever and amusing way.<br><br>  <br><br>_Example:_ Oscar Wilde was a master of the witty epigram, always turning conventional wisdom on its head.                                                   |
| **corollary**         | A proposition that follows from one already proved; a natural consequence or result.<br><br>  <br><br>_Example:_ If you value peace of mind, the natural corollary is that you must limit your desires.                                               |
| **sectarian**         | Denoting or concerning a sect or faction; narrow-minded or bigoted adherence to a specific group.<br><br>  <br><br>_Example:_ The country was torn apart by fierce sectarian violence between rival political factions.                               |
| **polemic**           | A strong verbal or written attack on someone or something.<br><br>  <br><br>_Example:_ The professor’s new book is a fierce polemic against the modern education system.                                                                              |
| **corrosive**         | Tending to cause something to be hurt or destroyed in a gradual way; physically or metaphorically eating away.<br><br>  <br><br>_Example:_ Unchecked anger has a corrosive effect on friendships, slowly ruining trust over time.                     |
| **assimilate**        | To take in, fully understand, and integrate information, ideas, or culture.<br><br>  <br><br>_Example:_ It takes time to assimilate the core principles of Stoicism into your daily routine.                                                          |
| **teleology**         | The explanation of phenomena by the purpose they serve rather than by their alleged causes (nature having a design).<br><br>  <br><br>_Example:_ In Stoic teleology, everything in the universe happens for a rational, ultimate good.                |
| **aphoristic**        | Concise, terse, and containing a concise statement of a principle or truth.<br><br>  <br><br>_Example:_ His writing style is highly aphoristic, making every single sentence feel like a standalone quote.                                            |
| **pious**             | Devoutly religious, or making a hypocritical display of virtue; dutiful to gods or family.<br><br>  <br><br>_Example:_ The emperor was widely praised for his pious devotion to the traditional gods of Rome.                                         |
| **satirize**          | To deride, mock, or criticize something or someone by using irony, sarcasm, or ridicule.<br><br>  <br><br>_Example:_ Late-night talk shows regularly satirize the absurdities of modern political campaigns.                                          |
| **merismos**          | A rhetorical device where a whole is broken down into its constituent parts for emphasis or detailed analysis.<br><br>  <br><br>_Example:_ When analyzing a problem, he used merismos to break the massive issue down into tiny, manageable steps.    |
| **paradigm**          | A typical example, pattern, or model of something.<br><br>  <br><br>_Example:_ The shift from geocentric to heliocentric models was a massive paradigm change in astronomy.                                                                           |
| **sage**              | The ideal, perfectly rational person who has attained complete wisdom and moral perfection.<br><br>  <br><br>_Example:_ For Stoics, the Sage is a theoretical ideal of virtue that one should always strive toward.                                   |
| **admonition**        | An act or action of admonishing; an authoritative counsel or warning.<br><br>  <br><br>_Example:_ She accepted her mentor's stern admonition with humility and changed her behavior.                                                                  |
| **vitriolic**         | Filled with bitter criticism or malice; sharply caustic.<br><br>  <br><br>_Example:_ The critic launched a vitriolic attack on the film, calling it an absolute waste of time.                                                                        |
| **dissimulate**       | To conceal or disguise one's thoughts, feelings, or character.<br><br>  <br><br>_Example:_ In the dangerous royal court, politicians had to dissimulate their true loyalties to survive.                                                              |
| **feigned**           | Simulated or pretended; insincere.<br><br>  <br><br>_Example:_ He listened to her lengthy explanation with feigned interest, secretly looking at the clock.                                                                                           |
